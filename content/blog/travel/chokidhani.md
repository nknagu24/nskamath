How often do we miss out on the authentic culture of a state because we do not get to experience the full culture and traditions? Cities have now modernized a lot, shifting their culture to incorporate more things to cater to the tourists’ needs. A notable instance was when I asked a cab driver in Shimla for suggestions to eating authentic Himachali dishes, he said they are not very popular among tourists. 

Of course, thinking about your income trumps maintaining culture and traditions. Now, the situation is a bit different: the higher-ups are slowly supporting traditional culture and arts. Traditions and culture is the personality of a region. And I understood that significance on a trip to Rajasthan, when I visited Chokhi Dhani.

Chokhi Dhani is a type of mock village which showcases the Rajasthani culture, food, and heritage. The name “*Chokhi Dhani”* literally translates to “Rajasthani Culture”. This was a tourism step taken in 1989 and spanned over 10 acres. The art, colours, folklore, and culture shines out.

The whole village is so beautiful and vibrant. The people are a little used to tourists, so they tended to be a bit non-committal. The night was cold, so we could enjoy roaming a lot more.

The dishes were delicious. They served white butter, bajre ka churma, gehu ka churma, kachori, sarson ka saag, Chole ki Chaat, Gatte ki Sabzi, Ker Sangdi, Alu Pyaz, Hari Mirch ka achaar, dal and baati, bajre ki roti, and khadi. The sweets were also amazing. Ker Sangdi piqued my interest a lot, because it was made of a scrub type of herb which grows natively in Rajasthan. I got more interested and realized that Ker Sangdi was made because of the lack of availability of crops.

The story was that during the great famine in India, when almost all vegetation had died off, there were certain small berries and long beans that surprisingly grew in abundance. When the locals cooked it, they loved its tangy and aromatic taste. Ker sangri is an assortment of five plant products — ker, sangri, babul fali (kumatiya), gunda and aamchur. It is also known as panchkuta because of this unique mix of five ingredients. Ker is a caper-like desert fruit, a berry that grows on a thorny shrub. It is tangy in taste, which also makes it a perfect ingredient for pickles. Sangri, on the other hand, are bean-like pods of the khejri tree below which the ker grew. Babul fali are the flat seeds of babul or acacia tree.

It seems to reduce the sourness, raw Ker berries are soaked in salt water, washed, and then dried under shade. Sangri beans are plucked when raw, boiled lightly, dried under shade, and preserved for almost a year.

This got me really interested in the cuisine of India. In a beautiful series called “*Raja, Rasoi Aur Anya Kahaniyaan*”, they explain it wonderfully. In that, they explain about the special red Mathania chillies they use in Laal-Maans; a red, spicy curry with mutton or chicken that is endemic to Rajasthan.

Chef Bulai Swain said that: “*Traditionally, laal maans was made with wild game meat (jungli maans), such as boar or deer and chillies were used to veil the gamy odour of the meat. It was a favourite among the royalties. While the spicy flavour is remained intact now, the meat used is tender mutton*.” It is predominantly in the mode of tamas and rajas.

What I found fascinating: I wondered how in such a hot place people could eat spicy items. Then the locals are seen explaining the concept. The heat from the chillies causes the person to sweat and helps cool down the bodies. This is the reason they are consumed.

The Mathania chillies are native to Rajasthan and have a Scoville pungency rating of medium. I like how local cuisine is so tuned to what is required for the people to tolerate the environment there. That fascinated me so much about the cuisine in India. All in all, Chokhi Dhani is a wonderful experience to experience just a teensy bit of true Rajasthan.

Again, I know locals who would disagree and say that it is just to attract tourists and cloak the culture as a mystical experience for tourists, but as a South Indian on a quick trip to Rajasthan; this was fascinating for sure.
