From the grey, skyscraper-filled London; a three hour bus ride on the reliable National Expressway took us through the meadows of the countryside to Cambridge. We had no clue what to expect; what could top the glitter of London?

We got down at the bus-stop- the iconic white Ferris wheel greeting us, and we hopped onto the local bus which took us to our Airbnb. After a brief respite, we set off to explore the heart of Cambridge.

The tarmac roads changed to cobblestone-paved footpaths as we approached the city centre. What greeted us was a sight to behold.

This view took our breath away. We were tired from the journey, tired from the walking, but our hearts sighed with an inexplicable emotion. We were looking at one of the great places of learning, of discoveries, of people working hard and achieving great milestones in academic education. We looked at students on bikes around us, some gathered in deep discussion, some tourists clicking pictures. As people in academia, we could feel the significance of what we saw in front of us, and it felt heavy.

After a few days of this conference, we got the last two days to roam around in Cambridge. We had a list: the Fitzwilliam Museum, the Backs, punting on the River Cam, Jack's Gelato, and to just saunter along the roads of the town.

We started with the [Fitzwilliam Museum](https://fitzmuseum.cam.ac.uk/), a long walk from our lodging.

The museum was quaint, but had everything. The collection was well-curated, beautifully arranged and had quiet pieces of history everywhere. It was wonderful, we never got bored, and every piece had information about the journey it took to reach the museum. We saw old paintings, and the famous Egyptian sarcophagi. There was a ceramics section that made one of us squeal with delight: it looked like it belonged in a doll house.

We shopped a lot at the museum store- beautiful Japanese painting decorated cards, an Escape Room puzzle, science-related postcards and magnets; we splurged. 

After this, we had to pass time until our punting booking. We passed streets and building that always looked like it was right out of a painting. We drained the charge on our mobile phones desperately clicking away, hoping to forever remember everything.

We then reached the banks of the River Cam, purchased a few beers, and got ready for our punting adventure with one of the groups there called [Let's Go Punting](https://letsgopunting.co.uk/).

Then, punting it was. We had about fifteen people in our boat, our punter was named Evan, who was about 19 and Scottish, and was a student at one of the colleges under the Cambridge umbrella. He also mentioned wryly that he wasn't supposed to actually mention that. He then took us on a historical journey.

He started with talking about the Backs: which has all the famous colleges in sight as we travelled across the River Cam. We passed the Bridge of Sighs: St John’s College where he explained that it was called so because when Queen Victoria visited in 1847 and remarked on its resemblance to the Bridge of Sighs in Venice, despite not actually resembling its Venetian counterpart. Another funny local legend about it was: the bridge connects student accommodation to the college's examination rooms, prompting students to let out "sighs" when crossing it to get their grades.


We then passed the Trinity College and Hall, which housed one of the most beautiful and biggest libraries. Trinity College was one of the largest and wealthiest of the Cambridge colleges, famous for its grand architecture. The Wren Library was built on a raised open-air cloister with books present from the first floor. This was done to protect the books inside from possible flooding. Evan also talked about Ramanujan and Newton attending this college. 

All this time, we were hooked onto his words. We quietly sipped beer, drinking in the views, the information, and just feeling overwhelmed by the sheer history the place held.

Next was Clare College. There were beautiful formal gardens and we passed the oldest bridge on the river (Clare Bridge) adorned with carved stone balls, and a picturesque wrought-iron gate. We craned our necks to take in every inch.

Then came the beautiful and classic King's College, both the college and chapel in view.  The river was serene, the weather just right.

Next up was the Queen's College, home to the famous Mathematical Bridge.  The Mathematical Bridge is a famous wooden footbridge at Queens' College, Cambridge, crossing the River Cam. Designed by [William Etheridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Etheridge) in 1748 and built in 1749, it is renowned for its ingenious engineering: it creates the illusion of a graceful arch, yet it is constructed entirely of straight pieces of timber. The brilliance of the bridge's design lies in its use of tangential and radial trussing as bending wood would've made it weaker.

One of the common myths, Evan said, was that Sir Isaac Newton had designed the bridge. He said for it to have been true, Sir Isaac Newton's ghost would have had to build it, as Newton died in 1727, more than 20 years before the bridge was built.

Evan had great many funny stories about the place. Prince Charles had attended Trinity College, and it was said that he couldn't get in, and it was only after his mother made the college take him in, he studied and graduated. I tried to cross-check this claim, however I couldn't find much about this on the internet. It must be a word-of-mouth story among the locals for sure. 

A recurring joke that was made at almost every historic stop were pointed digs to Oxford. Evan mentioned there had always been a rivalry between Oxford and Cambridge and he could relate to it, since he studies at one of the umbrella colleges under Cambridge [we all clapped at that]. Evan also had interesting information about famous graduation parties of the colleges, the St. John's graduation parties being famous.

There was a nice story for this too. It seems that during some hasty construction of this building, the type of bricks used for construction had changed halfway and this gave the wall a horrible patchy look. Hence, ivy was planted on the walls as a cover up. Evan also mentioned that this sort of gave rise to the name "Ivy-League", however when I cross-checked, it was a myth. We then took a group picture together, thanked him for his lovely punting session.

When the punting session ended, we disembarked and decided to get gelatos, and to excitedly discuss what he said and chuckle again. We strolled past the famous Jack's Gelato; known for their fresh and unconventionally flavoured gelatos.

As we strolled back to our lodgings, we passed another iconic Cambridge landmark: The Corpus Clock or the Stephen Hawking Clock.

[The Corpus Clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Clock) or Chronophage or the Grasshopper clock, is a large sculptural clock on the outside of the Taylor Library at Corpus Christi College, University of Cambridge. It is at street level at the junction of Bene't Street and Trumpington Street, looking out over King's Parade, Cambridge. The clock was engineered by and funded by John Taylor, an alumnus and honorary fellow of the college. It was officially unveiled to the public on 19 September 2008 by Cambridge physicist Stephen Hawking. The clock was named one of Time's Best Inventions of 2008.

We tried to figure it out, and it actually did show the seconds, minutes and hours in concentric circles. The clock's face is a rippling 24-carat gold-plated stainless steel disc, about 1.5 metres in diameter. Chronophage means "time-eater", and that creature moves its mouth, appearing to "eat up" the seconds as they pass, and occasionally it "blinks" in seeming satisfaction. The creature's constant motion produces an eerie grinding sound that suits its task. Below the clock is a Latin inscription: mundus transit et concupiscentia eius ("the world passeth away, and the lust thereof"). It seems that the clock is entirely accurate only once every five minutes. The rest of the time, the pendulum may seem to catch or stop, and the lights may lag or, then, race to get ahead. According to Taylor, this erratic motion reflects life's "irregularity". The Corpus Clock's clockwork is entirely mechanically controlled, without any computer programming, and electricity is used only to power a motor, which winds up the mechanism, and to power the blue LEDs that shine behind the slits in the clock's face.

After this majestic day, we decided to grab dinner at a local Turkish restaurant and called it a day, marking the end of our Cambridge journey. 

The whole experience of seeing both science and history intertwined was enough to give us goosebumps. The punting experience also showed us how history gave rise to the broadening of scientific horizons, how politics shaped the landscape of invention and discovery, and how the present Cambridge has retained both tradition and charm of the past, all the while moving steadily into the future. 

After all, that's what the Chronophage tells us to value.
