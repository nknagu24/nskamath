## Location of this UNESCO WHS

The **Historic Areas of Istanbul** occupy the historic peninsula on the European side of modern-day **Istanbul, Turkey**. Bordered by the **Golden Horn** to the north, the **Bosphorus** to the east, and the **Sea of Marmara** to the south, the peninsula has been one of the world's most strategically important locations for over two millennia. Its position at the crossroads of Europe and Asia made it a center of trade, culture, and political power.

## Founded by

The city traces its origins to the ancient Greek colony of **Byzantium**, founded in the **7th century BC**. In **324 CE**, the Roman emperor **Constantine the Great** chose Byzantium as the site of his new imperial capital. The city was rebuilt on a monumental scale and formally inaugurated in **330 CE** as **Constantinople**, also known as **New Rome**.

After the division of the Roman Empire, Constantinople became the capital of the **Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire** for more than a thousand years. Following its conquest by **Sultan Mehmed II** in **1453**, it became the capital of the **Ottoman Empire**. Although the Ottoman administration officially used the name **Kostantiniyye**, the name **Istanbul**, derived from the Greek phrase *eis tēn polin* ("to the city"), had already been in common use for centuries and eventually became the city's official name in the 20th century.

## Significance

Few cities in history have held as much political, cultural, and religious importance as Istanbul. For over **1,600 years**, it served as the capital of the **Byzantine Empire** and later the **Ottoman Empire**, shaping the history of Europe, the Middle East, and the Mediterranean.

The city's unique location on the **Bosphorus Strait** allowed it to control the only maritime passage between the **Black Sea** and the **Mediterranean**, making it one of history's most valuable commercial and military centers. As a meeting point between Europe and Asia, Istanbul became a melting pot of civilizations, where Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman traditions merged to create one of the world's richest architectural and cultural landscapes.

## Designation as a UNESCO WHS

The **Historic Areas of Istanbul** were inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** in **1985**. The designation recognizes the city's exceptional collection of Byzantine and Ottoman monuments, including masterpieces such as **Hagia Sophia**, **Topkapı Palace**, **Süleymaniye Mosque**, and the **Theodosian Walls**, which together illustrate more than fifteen centuries of continuous urban development and architectural achievement. 

## Byzantine Architecture

### Hagia Sophia

Originally built between **532 and 537 CE** by Emperor **Justinian I** after the destruction of two earlier churches during the Nika Revolt, **Hagia Sophia** is one of the greatest architectural achievements of the Byzantine world. Its enormous central dome, rising over 55 m above the floor, remained the largest in the world for nearly a thousand years and inspired countless later churches and mosques.

Following the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in **1453**, Sultan **Mehmed II** converted the cathedral into a mosque by adding minarets, a mihrab, and other Islamic features while preserving much of the original structure. In 1935, under the reforms of Mustafa Kemal Atatürk, Hagia Sophia was converted into a museum before being reconverted into a mosque in 2020.

### Hagia Irene

<div class="img-row-2-lp">
{{< img src="images/history/istanbul/hagiairene-1.webp" alt="hagiairene-1" >}}
{{< img src="images/history/istanbul/hagiairene-2.webp" alt="hagiairene-2" >}}
</div>

Located within the grounds of **Topkapı Palace**, **Hagia Irene** is one of the oldest surviving churches in Istanbul. Originally built during the reign of **Constantine the Great** and later rebuilt by **Justinian I** after the Nika Revolt, it served as Constantinople's cathedral before Hagia Sophia was completed.

Unlike Hagia Sophia, Hagia Irene was never converted into a mosque after the Ottoman conquest. Instead, it was used as an arsenal and later became one of the Ottoman Empire's first museums. It is especially famous for its rare **Iconoclastic mosaic cross** in the apse, one of the finest surviving examples of Byzantine Iconoclast art.

### Basilica Cistern


Constructed during the reign of **Justinian I** in the 6th century, the **Basilica Cistern** was built to store and supply fresh water to Constantinople's Great Palace and nearby monuments. Supported by **336 marble columns**, it is the largest surviving Byzantine cistern beneath Istanbul.

The cistern remained largely forgotten for centuries until it was rediscovered in the 16th century. Today it is one of Istanbul's most atmospheric monuments, famous for its mysterious underground setting and the two sculpted **Medusa heads** reused as column bases.

## The Fall of Constantinople

After withstanding numerous sieges for over a thousand years, Constantinople finally fell to the **Ottoman Sultan Mehmed II** on **29 May 1453** after a 53-day siege. The city's formidable **Theodosian Walls**, long considered impregnable, were breached with the aid of massive cannons designed by the Hungarian engineer **Orban**.

The fall of Constantinople marked the end of the **Byzantine Empire**, the last remnant of the Roman Empire, and ushered in the rise of the Ottoman Empire as a dominant world power. It also transformed Constantinople into the new Ottoman capital, beginning a new chapter in the city's long and remarkable history. 




## Ottoman Architecture

### Blue Mosque

Commissioned by **Sultan Ahmed I** and completed in **1617**, the **Blue Mosque (Sultan Ahmed Mosque)** was built directly opposite Hagia Sophia to symbolize the strength of the Ottoman Empire. It is renowned for more than **20,000 hand-painted İznik tiles**, whose blue patterns gave the mosque its popular name.

The mosque's six minarets were controversial because only the Prophet's Mosque in Medina had the same number. According to tradition, the controversy was resolved by adding a seventh minaret to the mosque in Medina, although another popular legend attributes the six minarets to a misunderstanding between the sultan and his architect.





### Grand Bazaar

{{< history-figure src="images/history/istanbul/grandbazar.webp" alt="Grand bazar" caption="" side="right" width="25%">}}

Established shortly after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople in the 15th century, the **Grand Bazaar** grew into one of the world's oldest and largest covered markets. Originally built to generate revenue for Hagia Sophia, it gradually expanded into a vast network of streets, shops, workshops, and caravanserais.

For centuries, each street specialized in a particular trade, creating a bustling commercial center that connected merchants from Europe, Asia, and the Middle East. Even today, the Grand Bazaar remains one of Istanbul's most iconic marketplaces.


### Topkapı Palace

{{< img src="images/history/istanbul/topkapi-1.webp" alt="topkapi-1" caption="" >}}




Built by **Sultan Mehmed II** after the Ottoman conquest of Constantinople, **Topkapı Palace** served as the residence of the Ottoman sultans and the administrative heart of the empire for nearly **400 years**. Perched on a promontory overlooking the Bosphorus, the palace occupies the site of the ancient Byzantine acropolis.

## Hippodrome of Constantinople

{{< history-figure src="images/history/istanbul/hippodrome-1.webp" alt="hippodrome" caption="" side="right" width="25%">}}

Originally built by **Septimius Severus** and greatly expanded by **Constantine the Great**, the **Hippodrome of Constantinople** was the social and political heart of the Byzantine capital. Inspired by Rome's Circus Maximus, it hosted spectacular chariot races, imperial ceremonies, public celebrations, and occasionally political uprisings.

After the Fourth Crusade in **1204**, the Hippodrome declined and was never rebuilt following the Ottoman conquest of 1453. Today, only a few monuments—including the **Obelisk of Theodosius**, the **Serpent Column**, and the **Walled Obelisk**—remain to mark the site of what was once one of the greatest stadiums of the ancient world.


## Religion

For more than a millennium, **Constantinople** was one of the most important centers of Christianity. Its bishop gradually became the **Ecumenical Patriarch of Constantinople**, the spiritual leader of the Eastern Orthodox Church and one of the highest-ranking bishops in Christendom. The city's growing prominence contributed to the theological and political tensions between the Eastern and Western Churches, culminating in the **Great Schism of 1054**, which permanently divided Christianity into the **Roman Catholic** and **Eastern Orthodox** traditions.

The city also holds great significance in **Islam**. Several sayings attributed to the Prophet Muhammad praise the future conquest of Constantinople, inspiring generations of Muslim rulers. After **Sultan Mehmed II** captured the city in **1453**, Constantinople became one of the most important centers of the Islamic world and remained the capital of the Ottoman Empire for nearly five centuries.

## Learning and Scholarship

Constantinople was also one of the leading intellectual centers of the medieval world. In **425 CE**, Emperor **Theodosius II** founded the **Pandidakterion**, often regarded as the Byzantine Empire's imperial university, where subjects such as philosophy, medicine, law, mathematics, and rhetoric were taught. Throughout the Byzantine era, the city's monasteries, libraries, and schools helped preserve and transmit the knowledge of classical Greece and Rome to later generations.

## Legacy

For over two millennia, Istanbul has stood at the meeting point of continents, cultures, and religions. Few cities have influenced world history as profoundly, serving as the capital of successive empires while preserving monuments that span Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman civilizations. Today, the Historic Areas of Istanbul preserve over two thousand years of continuous history, where Greek, Roman, Byzantine, and Ottoman monuments stand side by side in one of the world's greatest cultural landscapes.



## Interesting Facts

1. **Istanbul is the only major city in the world that spans two continents.** The **Bosphorus Strait** divides the city into European and Asian sides while connecting the **Black Sea** with the **Sea of Marmara**.

2. **The Tünel is one of the world's oldest underground railways.** Opened in **1875**, it is the second-oldest underground urban railway after the London Underground and is still in operation today.

3. **The legendary Orient Express ended in Istanbul.** The luxurious railway linking **Paris** and **Constantinople** became world-famous through **Agatha Christie's** novel *Murder on the Orient Express*. Christie is said to have written parts of the novel while staying at the historic **Pera Palace Hotel**.

4. **The Grand Bazaar is one of the oldest and largest covered markets in the world.** Dating back to the 15th century, it contains more than **4,000 shops** spread across **61 covered streets**, attracting millions of visitors each year.

5. **The word "turquoise" literally means "Turkish."** It comes from the Old French *turquois* ("Turkish") because the gemstone reached Europe through Ottoman trade routes. Ironically, the stone itself was mined mainly in Persia (modern-day Iran), not Turkey.

6. **Istanbul has served as the capital of three great empires.** Few cities in history can claim to have been the capital of the **Eastern Roman (Byzantine)**, **Latin**, and **Ottoman Empires**, making it one of the world's longest-serving imperial capitals.
