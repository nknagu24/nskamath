## Location of this UNESCO WHS

The **Acropolis of Athens** is an ancient citadel situated atop a flat-topped limestone hill rising about **150 m (490 ft)** above sea level in the heart of **Athens, Greece**. The complex contains some of the finest surviving monuments of Classical Greece, including the **Parthenon**, **Erechtheion**, **Temple of Athena Nike**, and the monumental gateway known as the **Propylaea**.

## Significance

{{< img src="images/history/acropolis/acropolis-card.webp" alt="Acropolis" caption="The Acropolis of Athena" >}}

The Acropolis is widely regarded as the greatest surviving symbol of ancient Greek civilization and the birthplace of Western classical architecture. Dedicated primarily to the goddess **Athena**, the patron deity of Athens, the monuments built during the 5th century BC represent the artistic, political, and cultural achievements of the Athenian Golden Age under **Pericles**. Their influence can still be seen in countless government buildings, museums, and memorials around the world.

## Designation as a UNESCO WHS

The **Acropolis of Athens** was inscribed as a **UNESCO World Heritage Site** in **1987**. UNESCO recognizes the Acropolis as one of humanity's greatest architectural and artistic achievements, whose monuments have profoundly influenced architecture, philosophy, politics, and the ideals of democracy for over two millennia.

## History

Archaeological evidence shows that the Acropolis has been inhabited since the **Neolithic period**, with the earliest settlements dating to around **4000 BC**. During the **Mycenaean Age** (c. 1600–1100 BC), the hill served as a heavily fortified royal citadel. Massive limestone fortification walls, known today as the **Cyclopean Walls**, were constructed to protect the palace complex. Around the 13th century BC, a natural fissure near the northeastern edge of the hill was converted into a well, providing a reliable source of drinking water during times of siege.

By the 6th century BC, the Acropolis had transformed from a military stronghold into the religious heart of Athens. Several sanctuaries dedicated to **Athena**, the city's patron goddess, were built on the hill. Around **490 BC**, following the Athenian victory at the **Battle of Marathon**, construction began on a grand marble temple known today as the **Older Parthenon**. This ambitious project, however, would never be completed.

In **480 BC**, the Persian king **Xerxes I** invaded Greece and captured Athens. The Acropolis was sacked, its temples were burned, and the unfinished Older Parthenon was destroyed. Rather than discard the ruined architectural fragments, the Athenians incorporated many of them into the northern defensive wall of the Acropolis, where they remain visible today as a lasting memorial to the Persian destruction.

{{< img src="images/history/acropolis/parthenon-1.webp" alt="parthenon 1" caption="Parthenon" >}}

The Acropolis was reborn during the **Golden Age of Athens** under the leadership of **Pericles**. Following the Greek victories over Persia, an ambitious rebuilding program was launched between **447 and 406 BC**. The sculptor **Phidias** oversaw the artistic decoration of the monuments, while architects **Ictinus**, **Callicrates**, and **Mnesicles** designed many of the iconic structures that define the Acropolis today. During this remarkable period, the **Parthenon**, **Propylaea**, **Temple of Athena Nike**, and **Erechtheion** were constructed, transforming the Acropolis into the architectural masterpiece that has inspired generations ever since.

## The Contest Between Athena and Poseidon

According to Greek mythology, both **Athena**, the goddess of wisdom, and **Poseidon**, the god of the sea, wished to become the patron deity of a newly founded city in Attica. To settle the dispute, the two gods descended from Mount Olympus to the Acropolis, where the city's people gathered to witness a contest. Each deity would present a gift, and the citizens would choose the one they considered the most valuable.

Poseidon stepped forward first. Raising his mighty trident, he struck the rock of the Acropolis with tremendous force. The ground split open, and a spring of water burst forth, drawing cheers from the crowd. Water was a precious resource, but the excitement quickly faded when the citizens discovered that the spring was salty and unsuitable for drinking. According to some versions of the myth, Poseidon's gift was instead the first horse on the earth.

Athena then quietly knelt and planted a seed into the ground. Almost instantly, an **olive tree** sprang to life. The people recognized that the tree would provide olives for food, oil for cooking and lamps, wood for tools and fuel, and a lasting symbol of peace and prosperity. Realizing that Athena's gift would benefit the city for generations, the citizens chose her as their patron goddess, and the city was named **Athens** in her honor.

The contest is closely associated with the **Erechtheion**, one of the most sacred temples on the Acropolis. The temple was built over several revered sites linked to the legend, including the marks believed to have been left by Poseidon's trident and the sacred olive tree of Athena. Although the original tree no longer survives, an olive tree growing beside the Erechtheion is traditionally regarded as its descendant and remains one of the symbolic highlights of the Acropolis today.

The **Parthenon** is the largest and most celebrated monument on the Acropolis. Dedicated to **Athena Parthenos** ("Athena the Virgin"), it was constructed between **447 and 432 BC** under the leadership of **Pericles**. Besides serving as a temple, it also housed the treasury of the Delian League and contained the colossal **Athena Parthenos** statue created by the sculptor **Phidias**.



### Erechtheion

{{< img src="images/history/acropolis/erechtheion-1.webp" alt="erechtheion-1" >}}

The **Erechtheion**, built between **421 and 406 BC**, is one of the most unusual temples in the Greek world. Unlike the perfectly symmetrical Parthenon, it was designed with multiple levels to accommodate several sacred sites associated with Athenian mythology. It is best known for its **Porch of the Caryatids**, where six sculpted female figures replace conventional columns.

### Propylaea

The **Propylaea** served as the monumental gateway to the Acropolis. Designed by the architect **Mnesicles**, construction began in **437 BC**. Visitors entering the sacred hill passed through this impressive marble entrance before reaching the temples beyond.

### Temple of Athena Nike

Standing beside the Propylaea is the **Temple of Athena Nike**, a small yet elegant temple dedicated to Athena in her aspect as the goddess of victory. Built between **427 and 424 BC**, it commemorated Athenian military successes during the Persian Wars and once housed a statue of **Nike Apteros** ("Wingless Victory"), symbolizing that victory would never leave Athens.

### Athena Promachos

Between the Propylaea and the Parthenon once stood the colossal bronze statue of **Athena Promachos** ("Athena who fights in the front line"), created by **Phidias** around **450 BC**. Standing nearly **9 m (30 ft)** tall on its pedestal, the statue dominated the Acropolis. Ancient writers claimed that the gilded tip of Athena's spear could be seen by sailors approaching Athens from far out at sea.

## Architecture of the Parthenon

The Parthenon is regarded as the finest example of **Classical Greek architecture** and the culmination of the **Doric order**. Constructed almost entirely from **Pentelic marble**, the temple measures approximately **69.5 m × 30.9 m** and is surrounded by **46 outer columns**. Despite its apparent simplicity, nearly every part of the structure was carefully engineered to create the illusion of perfect symmetry.

One of the Parthenon's most remarkable features is the use of **optical refinements**. The stylobate (the platform on which the columns stand) is not perfectly flat but curves slightly upward toward the center. Likewise, the columns are not perfectly straight—they bulge gently at the middle, a technique known as **entasis**, and lean subtly inward. Even the corner columns are slightly thicker than the others. These deliberate refinements compensate for optical distortions, making the temple appear perfectly straight and harmonious to the human eye.

The temple was richly decorated with sculptural masterpieces depicting scenes from Greek mythology, including the **Gigantomachy**, **Centauromachy**, **Amazonomachy**, and the **Trojan War**. Running around the upper walls of the inner sanctuary was a continuous Ionic frieze portraying the **Panathenaic Procession**, one of the most important religious festivals of ancient Athens.

At the heart of the Parthenon stood the colossal **Athena Parthenos**, a chryselephantine (gold-and-ivory) statue created by the sculptor **Phidias** around **438 BC**. Standing approximately **12 m (39 ft)** tall, the statue depicted Athena wearing a golden helmet and holding **Nike**, the winged goddess of victory, in her outstretched right hand. Although the original statue has been lost, it is known from ancient descriptions and later copies, and remains one of the greatest artistic achievements of the ancient world.


## Later History

Following the decline of the ancient Greek world, the Acropolis continued to evolve under successive civilizations. During the Byzantine period, the **Parthenon** was converted into a Christian church dedicated to the **Virgin Mary** and later served as a Roman Catholic cathedral under Frankish rule. After the Ottoman conquest of Athens in **1458**, it was transformed into a mosque, with the addition of a minaret. Despite these changes, the structure remained remarkably well preserved for nearly two thousand years.

The monument suffered its greatest destruction in **1687** during the **Morean War**, when Venetian forces besieged Ottoman-held Athens. The Ottomans had stored gunpowder inside the Parthenon, believing the ancient temple would not be targeted. A Venetian artillery shell struck the building, triggering a massive explosion that destroyed much of the roof and central structure. Many of the sculptures were severely damaged, leaving the Parthenon in the ruined state seen today.

In the early 19th century, **Lord Elgin**, the British ambassador to the Ottoman Empire, removed a significant portion of the surviving sculptural decoration from the Parthenon. These sculptures, now known as the **Elgin Marbles**, are housed in the **British Museum** and remain the subject of an ongoing international debate regarding their return to Greece. Today, extensive conservation and restoration work continues to preserve the Acropolis for future generations.

## A Full-Scale Replica in Nashville

{{< img src="images/history/acropolis/nashville-3.webp" alt="nashville-3" >}}


Although the original Parthenon survives only as a ruin, the world's only **full-scale replica** stands in **Nashville, Tennessee, USA**. Built in **1897** for the Tennessee Centennial Exposition, the replica was originally constructed from temporary materials but was later rebuilt in permanent concrete between **1920 and 1931**.

Known as the **"Athens of the South,"** Nashville chose the Parthenon as the centerpiece of its exposition to reflect the city's strong educational traditions. Today, the replica serves as an art museum and houses a remarkable reconstruction of the **Athena Parthenos** statue. Completed by sculptor **Alan LeQuire** in **1990**, the 13-meter-tall gilded statue offers visitors one of the closest approximations of how the original interior of the Parthenon may have appeared over 2,400 years ago.

## Interesting Facts

1. **The Acropolis was once brilliantly colorful.** Contrary to its white appearance today, the temples were originally painted in vivid shades of blue, red, green, and gold. Traces of these ancient pigments can still be detected on some sculptures using modern imaging techniques.

2. **The Parthenon served as Athens' treasury.** Besides being a temple dedicated to Athena, it also housed the wealth of the Delian League, effectively making it one of the most secure vaults in the ancient Greek world.

3. **No line in the Parthenon is perfectly straight.** The platform curves gently upward, the columns bulge slightly at their centers (entasis), lean subtly inward, and the corner columns are slightly thicker. These deliberate refinements correct optical illusions, making the temple appear perfectly symmetrical.

4. **The Caryatids seen today are replicas.** The six sculpted maidens supporting the porch of the Erechtheion are modern copies. Five of the original statues are displayed in the nearby Acropolis Museum, while the sixth was removed by Lord Elgin in the early 19th century and is currently housed in the British Museum.

5. **The Acropolis preserves traces of its ancient destruction.** Fragments of the temples destroyed during the Persian invasion of 480 BC were intentionally built into the northern defensive wall of the Acropolis. They remain visible today as a permanent memorial to the city's destruction and rebirth.

6. **An olive tree still grows beside the Erechtheion.** According to Greek mythology, it is regarded as the descendant of the sacred olive tree planted by Athena during her contest with Poseidon for the patronage of Athens.

7. **The Parthenon has had many lives.** Over the last 2,500 years, it has served as a pagan temple, a Christian church, a Roman Catholic cathedral, an Ottoman mosque, a gunpowder magazine, and finally one of the world's most studied archaeological monuments.

8. **The original Athena Parthenos no longer exists.** The colossal gold-and-ivory statue created by Phidias disappeared during late antiquity. The most faithful reconstruction can now be seen in the full-scale replica of the Parthenon in Nashville, Tennessee.
