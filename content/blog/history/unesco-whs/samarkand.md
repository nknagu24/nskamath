## Location of this UNESCO WHS site

Samarkand is a historic city in southeastern ***Uzbekistan*** and is among the oldest continuously inhabited cities in Central Asia. Situated in the fertile Zerafshan River valley, it flourished for over two millennia as one of the principal cities of the Silk Road.

## Significance

{{< history-figure src="images/history/samarkand/timur.webp" alt="Timur" caption="Timur (Tamerlane)" side="right" width="20%">}}

For centuries, Samarkand served as one of the great crossroads of civilizations. Located along the Silk Road, it connected China, Central Asia, Persia, and the Mediterranean, facilitating the exchange of goods, ideas, religions, science, and art. Its greatest period of prosperity came under **Amir Timur (Tamerlane)**, who transformed the city into the capital of the Timurid Empire during the 14th century.

## Designation as a UNESCO WHS

*Samarkand – Crossroads of Cultures* was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in **2001**. UNESCO recognizes the city as an exceptional example of the cultural exchanges that took place along the Silk Road, where Persian, Central Asian, Islamic, and later Timurid traditions came together to create one of the world's most influential urban centers.

## Etymology

The city was known as **Marakanda (Markanda)** when it was conquered by Alexander the Great in **329 BC**.

The origin of the modern name *Samarkand* remains debated. One widely accepted explanation traces it to the Sogdian words **samar** ("stone" or "rock") and **kand** ("fort" or "town"), giving the meaning **"stone fort"** or **"rocky town."**

An alternative explanation comes from the 11th-century scholar **Mahmud al-Kashghari**, who recorded the Karakhanid Turkic name **Sämizkänd**, meaning **"fat city"**—a reference to the city's wealth and prosperity.

## Historical Overview

Archaeological evidence indicates that settlements existed in the Samarkand region as early as **1500 BC**. The ancient city of **Afrasiab**, founded around the 7th century BC, became one of the most important cities of Sogdiana before being destroyed during the Mongol invasion led by **Genghis Khan** in the 13th century.

Samarkand experienced its golden age after **Amir Timur** made it the capital of his empire in **1370**. Determined to build the finest city in the Islamic world, Timur brought architects, artisans, scholars, and craftsmen from across his vast empire. During this period, many of Samarkand's iconic monuments—including **Gur-e Amir**, **Bibi-Khanym Mosque**, **Shah-i-Zinda**, and the early structures of the **Registan**—were constructed.

The Timurid architectural style developed in Samarkand later influenced Islamic architecture far beyond Central Asia. Its monumental domes, glazed tilework, geometric decoration, and grand proportions inspired later architectural traditions, including those of the Safavids in Persia, the Ottomans, and the Mughals in India.

Today, the UNESCO World Heritage Site preserves layers of Samarkand's history, from the archaeological remains of ancient Afrasiab to the Timurid monuments and the Russian colonial quarter developed during the 19th century.

## Gur-e Amir

{{< img src="images/history/samarkand/gure-amir1.webp" alt="Gur-e Amir" caption="" >}}

Gur-e Amir, meaning **"Tomb of the King"** in Persian, is the mausoleum of **Amir Timur (Tamerlane)** and one of the masterpieces of Timurid architecture.

<div class="img-row-2-lp">
{{< img src="images/history/samarkand/gure-amir2.webp"  alt="Gur-e Amir 2" >}}
{{< img src="images/history/samarkand/gure-amir3.webp" alt="Gur-e Amir 3" >}}
</div>

Although Timur wished to be buried in his birthplace of **Shahrisabz**, he was instead interred in Samarkand after his death in 1405. According to tradition, heavy snowfall blocked the route to Shahrisabz, making burial there impossible.

The elegant ribbed blue dome, richly decorated interior, and harmonious proportions of Gur-e Amir became a model for later Islamic funerary architecture. Historians widely regard it as one of the architectural inspirations for later Mughal mausoleums, including the Taj Mahal.

## Some Interesting Myths and Facts

1. Amir Timur believed that monumental architecture reflected the strength and prestige of an empire. At the entrance of his summer palace, **Ak-Saray** in Shahrisabz, an inscription famously proclaims:

   > *"If you doubt our strength, look at the buildings we have built."*

2. The tomb of Timur is associated with one of Central Asia's most famous legends—the **Curse of Timur**.

   - **Nader Shah and the jade slab:** During the 18th century, Nader Shah of Persia reportedly removed the large jade slab covering Timur's tomb. Soon afterward, he is said to have suffered a series of misfortunes, including the sudden illness of his son. Following the advice of his advisers, he returned the stone to Samarkand, after which the misfortunes reportedly ceased.

   - **The Soviet excavation and the curse:** In **June 1941**, Soviet anthropologists opened Timur's tomb under orders from Stalin to study his remains and verify that the tomb indeed belonged to Timur. According to local legend, disturbing the tomb would unleash disaster. Just days after the excavation, **Operation Barbarossa** began as Nazi Germany invaded the Soviet Union on **22 June 1941**, leading many to associate the invasion with the supposed curse.

     Timur's remains were reburied with Islamic rites in **November 1942**. A few weeks later, the Soviet Union achieved its decisive victory at the **Battle of Stalingrad**, giving rise to another popular legend that the curse had finally been lifted. Although historians regard these events as coincidence, the story remains one of Samarkand's most enduring legends.
